A customer wants to return a pair of headphones. The agent finds the order, checks the date, and offers a return. Everything looks normal. There is just one problem: the company changed its return policy yesterday.

The model is the same. The prompt is the same. The business is not.

That is a useful way to approach the question behind “Databricks for agents.” A production agent needs a current view of the business, permission to act, a record of what happened, and a way to improve. Observing a model call is one part of that system. The more consequential question is whether we can connect an agent’s decision to its business outcome, diagnose a failure, and test a better version.

Let’s make that concrete with a retail agent. We will follow one request through context, tools, traces, training, serving, and evaluation. Along the way, you can change the rules yourself.

I work at Databricks. This is my technical interpretation and proposed experiment, not a customer case study or an official performance claim. Product availability is dated 31 October 2026; source links and preview boundaries are included below.

01 / The business is an input

The rule changes.
The weights do not.

Consider a deliberately simple policy: returns were allowed within 30 days; a new policy allows 14. For this toy retailer, the current policy applies to every request, including older orders. A real retailer might grandfather earlier purchases. That applicability rule must also be explicit.

A request on day 21 separates two questions: does the agent have the current rule, and does it use that rule correctly? The first is a context problem. The second may be a reasoning, training, or workflow problem. Changing the model cannot reliably repair missing information.

Inclusive boundary: day 14 is eligible under the 14-day rule. All other eligibility conditions are assumed satisfied. The simulator always follows the policy it receives; real models may not.

Notice what happens when you disable the write guard. A mistaken proposal becomes a mistaken transaction. With the guard enabled, the database remains correct, but the agent still fails to resolve the request correctly. We need to measure both attempted and executed violations.

In an operational system, the tool should enforce authorization, applicable policy, allowed item scope, and idempotency. A language model’s “yes” must not become permission. The agent should fetch the applicable rule, explain the result, and seek an approved exception when the workflow allows one. An old conversation remembered by the agent must not outrank current authoritative policy.

02 / A trace becomes a business record

A polite answer is not
a successful transaction.

“Your return is confirmed” is easy to grade as helpful. It is also possible for the tool call to fail, target the wrong item, create a duplicate, or bypass an approval. To evaluate the task, inspect what changed, what was permitted to change, and whether the user’s request was actually completed.

The original τ-bench makes this idea useful for research: agents talk to simulated users, follow domain rules, use tools, and are evaluated against database outcomes. Retail is a particularly legible example because the side effects are familiar. The open benchmark gives us a task environment, not evidence that our proposed Databricks implementation already works.

A blocked write is useful safety evidence. It does not, by itself, prove task success or a good customer experience.

This is where observability grows beyond debugging. A trace can tell us what policy the agent saw. An action ledger tells us what the tool committed. A reviewed outcome tells us whether that action satisfied the task. Their join identifies the intervention: refresh context, repair a tool contract, collect examples of a repeated decision error, or investigate serving latency.

MLflow’s documented workflow connects traces, assessments, evaluation datasets, and monitoring. Traces stored under Unity Catalog can support broader analysis. Our proposed application still has to carry the identifiers and write the outcome records; the platform does not magically infer business correctness. [5] [6]

The minimal record that makes an investigation reproducible

Persist task_id, trial_id, trace_id, customer authorization scope, policy ID and effective version, dataset revision, prompt/harness revision, model checkpoint and adapter, endpoint deployment, tool requests, committed mutations, and evaluator revision. Redact sensitive payloads and restrict access to the retained record.

Use request-level joins only where identifiers exist. GPU utilization and queue metrics may be aggregated; compare aligned time windows rather than inventing a per-request GPU measurement.

03 / Connect the responsibilities

Three runtimes.
One improvement loop.

“The runtime” is an ambiguous phrase. The agent application needs execution, state, and tool access. Training needs a GPU environment and reproducible data. Inference needs a loaded model, batching, memory, and a service boundary. These are different jobs with different failure modes.

For this design, Agent Runtime hosts the application, AI Runtime supports adaptation and GPU experimentation, and Model Serving hosts the custom inference endpoint. Unity Catalog governs the analytical artifacts. The operational action ledger must preserve transaction semantics; Lakebase is a candidate for the custom retail extension. The interesting asset is the connected record. A failed scenario becomes an inspected trace. A reviewed trace may become a training example. A training run produces a checkpoint. A deployed checkpoint is evaluated against the same task definitions and new business conditions. Every transition needs an owner, a version, and a quality gate.

Some failures should never enter a training queue. A missing permission belongs in access control. An outdated rule belongs in context retrieval. A malformed database transaction belongs in the tool implementation. Specialization earns its place only after a repeated, learnable behavior remains.

The open-model angle matters because it adds a controllable intervention. We can hold the model family constant and adapt its behavior, inspect its serving configuration, and compare its economics with a frontier reference under the same task conditions. “Open-weight” describes access to weights; it does not automatically mean every model has an unrestricted open-source license.

04 / The dataset is a starting point

11,395 records.
How many usable examples?

For a candidate training source, inclusionAI’s AReaL-tau2-data is unusually relevant: multi-turn, tool-using interactions in the τ² environment family. Its publisher reports 11,395 retail SFT records and 563 retail RL records. SFT records represent assistant turns with context, not 11,395 independent conversations. The release includes environment data and source identifiers. These are publisher counts, not the result of a local audit. [3]

That distinction changes how we split the data. Randomly separating turns can put the beginning of a dialogue in training and its ending in evaluation. Even dialogue-level separation can leak near-identical scenario families. We need lineage, compatibility checks, and a held-out challenge constructed after the checkpoint is frozen.

Teach a bounded skill on AI Runtime

The first adaptation should be a modest supervised fine-tuning baseline, such as LoRA where the selected model and recipe support it. Train the model to identify the right entities, sequence tool calls, use supplied policy, ask for missing information, and communicate a tool’s actual result. Do not train yesterday’s return window as a timeless truth.

AIR became generally available on 21 October 2026. Its role here is managed GPU experimentation and training in the Databricks environment. The NVIDIA integration provides a route through supported NeMo tooling and containers. We still choose and validate a recipe compatible with our checkpoint, architecture, tokenizer, and hardware. A supported container is not evidence that an arbitrary recipe is correct. [8] [10]

Start with a small tool-capable open model whose license and serving support fit the project. Qwen3.5-4B is a candidate because the custom-serving documentation includes a starter. That is a deployment foothold, not an assertion of retail accuracy. Lock the base revision and chat template, then log the adapter configuration, sequence length, optimizer, learning rate, seed, training steps, validation set, and budget. Compare the original and adapted checkpoint under identical harness settings. [9]

During training, supervise the visible assistant messages and tool calls in the format the chosen model expects. Do not assume the dataset’s separate reasoning field is required. Review the loss masking: training on system prompts or user content accidentally can turn an apparently working job into the wrong learning objective. Save checkpoints and validation reports together with the dataset manifest.

Reinforcement learning is a second experiment. The simulator can provide verifiable outcomes, but a reward can still be incomplete or exploitable. Before optimizing it, test wrong items, duplicate writes, prohibited actions later reversed, and tasks with several required steps. AIR’s examples include post-training workflows; KARL offers research precedent, not proof that managed custom RL is generally available for this project. [11] [12]

05 / Make the conclusion earn its place

Did the model improve,
or did we finally tell it the rule?

A before-and-after comparison can conceal the most important variable. If the adapted model also receives a better prompt, fresher context, and a different tool implementation, we cannot attribute the improvement to training. Keep those factors separate.

First evaluate the unchanged business. Then freeze checkpoints, create a sealed policy-change test, and cross two factors: original versus specialized weights, stale versus current context. Add a frontier model with the same current information as a practical reference. The stale conditions are diagnostic controls, not a production configuration.

No colored accuracy heatmap until measurements exist. A real result needs task counts, repeated trials, uncertainty, and violation rates.

Two tracks, two honest claims

Track A is benchmark reproduction. Pin the canonical engine, tasks, policies, tools, simulator model and settings, and reward implementation. Instrument without changing semantics. Report the exact release and task inventory. Sierra’s repository distinguishes benchmark generations; scores from different generations are not automatically comparable. [4]

Track B is our adaptation study. The 30-to-14-day change, authorization probes, operational ledger, and workload tests belong here. They are custom extensions, not official τ-bench results. Keep the canonical simulator for Track A; independently validate any Lakebase-backed tool implementation used in Track B.

Repeat tasks with documented seeds and settings. At least three trials per task is a useful first comparison, not a universal guarantee of statistical power. Report uncertainty at the task or scenario-family level; repeated runs are correlated observations. If reporting passk, explain consistent success across attempts rather than confusing it with finding one successful attempt among many.

The result must be allowed to disappoint us. If current context explains most of the improvement, lead with that. If specialization lowers cost but increases obsolete-policy errors, it is not ready. If the frontier reference wins on quality and total cost, that is a useful deployment decision. A platform argument becomes stronger when it helps us discover these answers, rather than predetermining one.

06 / The endpoint has a bill

Open weights.
Very real capacity.

A successful training run produces an artifact. A successful service answers requests under the expected workload. Package the checkpoint or adapter, tokenizer, chat template, tool parser, inference dependencies, and vLLM configuration as a release. Test the exact environment before promoting it. The application and inference endpoint remain separate deployments.

The documented custom LLM route tests vLLM in an AIR notebook and packages that environment for Model Serving. As of this article’s date, this route is in Public Preview, with cloud and region constraints and no dynamic autoscaling. Verify workspace access and current limitations before committing to an architecture. Do not assume every Unity Gateway feature works identically on every endpoint type. [9] [13]

Evaluate low traffic, sustained traffic, and a burst separately. Record queue time, time to first token, throughput, end-to-end latency, and failures at the tested concurrency. Check structured tool calls and long contexts. Quantization, batch settings, output limits, and cache configuration can affect both behavior and throughput; hold them fixed in the model-adaptation comparison, then study them separately.

At low volume, idle capacity can dominate. At high volume, insufficient capacity can make an apparently cheap endpoint unusable. The calculator below isolates the first effect. It does not predict throughput or declare that a single GPU can handle the selected workload.

At these assumptions, the specialist needs more traffic to amortize fixed capacity.

Specialist cost = (GPU rate × provisioned hours + amortized training/data + tasks × other cost) ÷ (tasks × success rate). Reference cost = all-in cost per attempt ÷ success rate. Put retries, tools, escalation, and evaluation into the appropriate all-in inputs. Capacity is not modeled.

The denominator is doing important work. A model that finishes fewer tasks can cost more per success even if each inference is cheaper. Keep failed attempts in the numerator. For a production study, also report the marginal online cost so readers can distinguish an inference improvement from an amortization assumption.

Promotion requires more than beating the reference at one slider setting. Establish an error budget, regression suite, workload envelope, and rollback path. Route a limited traffic slice to a candidate only after offline checks pass. Sample live traces for evaluation, inspect shifts in task mix, and reconcile proposed actions with committed outcomes. MLflow production monitoring is documented as Beta; use that status in the operational plan. [14]

From design to an executed study

Build the loop in
seven verifiable steps.

  1. Prove one complete request.

    Freeze a compatible simulator, connect one candidate endpoint, run a task, and correlate its trace with its verified outcome. Resolve tool schemas and chat formatting before scaling.

  2. Freeze and audit the data.

    Publish revisions, hashes, license records, grouping rules, overlap checks, and retained/quarantined counts. Do not call a public benchmark uncontaminated merely because our fine-tuning split is clean.

  3. Establish the original baseline.

    Run the canonical track unchanged and the custom track separately. Record simulator, model, prompt, tool, and evaluator settings. Review representative failures before selecting an intervention.

  4. Train one bounded adaptation.

    Run the SFT baseline on AIR with a fixed budget. Save the original checkpoint, adapter, recipe, environment, data manifest, and validation results as one traceable experiment.

  5. Introduce the sealed business change.

    Freeze checkpoints first. Generate reviewed scenarios around the new boundary, exceptions, permissions, and composite requests. Run the factorial comparison with comparable information and budgets.

  6. Measure the service, not just the model.

    Deploy the tested artifact to the supported endpoint. Measure latency and cost under declared traffic regimes. Include idle capacity and unsuccessful attempts.

  7. Publish the evidence and the exceptions.

    Release configurations, task-level outcomes where permitted, aggregate uncertainty, costs, and failure cases. Promote only a version that meets the declared quality and operational criteria.

That is the stronger meaning of “Databricks for agents”: a place to connect enterprise context, action, observation, and controlled adaptation. AIR and open-model serving make the learning part concrete. Governed data and verifiable outcomes make it accountable.

The architecture is available to investigate. The performance case still has to be earned, one controlled experiment at a time.

Evidence status. This article implements six browser explorations and specifies a proposed experiment. It does not deploy a Databricks agent, audit the downloaded training data, train a model, or report a benchmark run. Runtime access, compatible pinned releases, region, hardware, and compute budget must be resolved before execution. The simulations and calculator are deliberately separated from empirical results.

Reproducibility boundary. The public data may overlap with benchmark tasks or with a base model’s pretraining. Source-level filtering reduces avoidable leakage; it cannot prove an unknown pretraining corpus is clean. The custom post-freeze change helps test adaptation, but it is not a substitute for broader generalization evidence.

Interface note. The exploration-first approach was inspired by David Aerne’s computational design work. All diagrams, controls, and article code here are original implementations. No customer data is used; the controls run locally in your browser.

Implementation notes for the browser explorations

The policy panel evaluates a simple inclusive date rule and a separate write guard. The trace, architecture, audit, and experiment panels reveal authored explanatory content. The calculator applies the displayed accounting formula to user-entered assumptions. None connects to an inference endpoint, uses dataset samples as live outputs, or estimates model accuracy. Changing one panel does not alter another.
